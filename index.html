How this tool works

The tool compares a temperature and humidity reading, or a whole logger record, with published research on how collections deteriorate. It rates each cause of damage separately. It is a screening aid that shows where to look more closely. It does not replace conservator advice.

Terms in plain words

  • Relative humidity (RH): how much water vapour the air holds compared with the most it could hold at that temperature. Warm air holds more, so RH drops when a room warms, even if no moisture has left.
  • Dew point: the temperature at which the air would be saturated and water would condense on a cold surface. It follows the actual moisture in the air, so it stays put when a room warms or cools while RH moves.
  • Moisture content (EMC): the water, as a percentage of its weight, that a wood-like material settles at when held at a given temperature and RH.
  • Lifetime multiplier: how long a material lasts compared with steady 20 °C and 50% RH. 2× means twice as long. 0.5× means half as long.
  • PI and TWPI: the Image Permanence Institute’s Preservation Index is one figure for how slowly organic material decays at steady conditions. Higher is better. TWPI is the same figure averaged over a period of changing conditions.
  • Activation energy: how strongly the speed of ageing depends on temperature. A higher value means cooling helps more.

How to read the ratings

Each cause of damage is rated Good, Caution or Risk. It shows N/A where the material profile says the cause does not apply. IPI calls its middle band OK and CCI (the Canadian Conservation Institute) calls its middle band Watch. Both show here as Caution, except for mechanical damage, where IPI’s only passing band shows as OK. Rows marked Indicator give context and carry no rating.

The checks

  • Chemical ageing. Heat and humidity speed up the slow chemical breakdown of paper, textile, film and other organic material. The rating uses IPI’s PI: 75 or more is Good, 45 to under 75 is Caution, and under 45 is Risk. The lifetime multiplier comes from five published models. They agree between 30% and 60% RH and drift apart outside that range, where there is little data.
  • Mechanical damage. Wood, paper, leather and similar materials take up and lose moisture as humidity changes, so they swell and shrink. Too dry or too damp strains them. The check uses the moisture content they would settle at. The comfortable range is 5 to 12.5%. IPI has no Good band for this cause, so in range is rated OK and outside it is Risk. A single reading cannot show fluctuation. The Logged data tab can.
  • Mould. The rating follows CCI data on how long mould takes to start at each RH. Below 65% RH, spores do not germinate (start to grow) within 1000 days. Germination takes about 600 days at 65%, 50 days at 70%, 12 days at 75% and 1.5 days at 80%. Visible growth takes longer: about 500, 130, 35 and 12 days at the same points. These figures apply to surfaces rich in protein, starch or sugar, such as leather, skin, parchment and sized or soiled textile. For those, Risk starts at 70% RH and Caution at 65%, widened by your safety margin. For clean paper, textile and wood, CCI puts visible growth at about 80 to 85% RH, so Risk starts at 80%. CCI fitted its figures near 25 °C and treats them as cautious at other temperatures. A sustained drop below 65% RH ends the growth cycle. A second model, Sedlbauer’s, puts the limit near 76% RH at 20 °C. It is shown for reference and does not set the rating, because it is less cautious for museum materials.
  • Metal corrosion. Corrosion speeds up as moisture rises, sharply above 75% RH. The rating uses moisture content: under 7.0% is Good, 7.0 to under 10.5% is OK, and 10.5% or more is Risk. Salts left on salt-contaminated or archaeological metal absorb water at low RH (calcium chloride at 33%, magnesium chloride at 35%). For these, Risk starts at 33% and Caution at 30%, the typical critical RH CCI gives for special metal collections.
  • Gelatin blocking. CCI advises keeping RH below 60% so photographic gelatin layers do not stick together. Above 60% is Caution and 75% or more is Risk.

Control types (ASHRAE, as summarised by CCI)

These are ready-made specifications for how tightly to hold temperature and RH. CCI calls them types of control, not classes, because AA is not better than D. They are different specifications at different costs. Each has four parts. Outer limits are the range that must never be exceeded. The annual baseline is the long-run average the space holds. For a permanent collection, that is the building’s own historic average, which you set above. Seasonal change is the slow, planned drift from the baseline over the year. Short-term change is the swing allowed over hours or days. This tool checks the outer limits and the short-term swing. It does not check seasonal change.

Material profiles

A profile switches off checks that do not apply to a material. It also sets the starting lifetime class and activation energy, which you can change. Leather, skin, parchment and sized or soiled textile use the sensitive mould bands. Clean paper, textile and wood use the 80% mould limit. Photographic prints and film use the sensitive mould bands and add gelatin blocking. Clean iron and copper alloys are checked for corrosion only. Salt-contaminated or archaeological metal uses the salt limits above. Plastics, rubber and foam use the 30-year class and 70 kJ/mol (CCI’s range for polyurethane and resin varnish is 60 to 80). The lifetime classes of 1000, 300, 100 and 30 years are CCI’s, and each can be out by up to three times.

Not included: unstable glass (CCI’s text and table disagree on which side of about 40% and 55% RH causes weeping and crizzling), lead, zinc and bismuth alloys (they depend on air pollutants), and minerals such as pyrite (the sources read gave no figures).

Logged data

Load a logger CSV. Nothing leaves your browser. The tool finds the time, temperature and humidity columns from their headings. Dates are read as day/month/year unless a number above 12 appears in the middle position, which switches to month/day/year. Times are used as recorded. The tool reports the typical interval between readings and the largest gap.

  • Mould runs: a run is a stretch at or above 65% RH. A dip below 65% of up to 3 hours is ignored, because a short dip does not dry the material. A longer dip ends the run, following CCI. Each run is compared with CCI’s germination and visible-growth times at its peak RH.
  • Chemical ageing: adds up ageing over the period as equivalent time at steady 20 °C and 50% RH, using the same form as the headline lifetime multiplier. TWPI averages PI over the period and gives the worst conditions the most weight.
  • Mechanical damage and metal corrosion: time spent in each moisture band. Mechanical damage is Risk if more than 5% of the time is outside 5 to 12.5%, and OK otherwise. Metal corrosion is Risk if more than 0.5% of the period is damp (75% RH or more) or in the highest moisture band.
  • Control type check: time within the short-term swing around your baseline, and time outside the outer limits.
  • Condensation: hours at 95% RH or more and at 90% or more, plus the highest dew point recorded.
  • Pest indicators: time below 10 °C, from 10 to 15 °C, from 15 to 20 °C and above 20 °C. Clothes moths stop laying eggs below about 10 °C, breed less below about 15 °C, and can produce up to three generations a year above 20 °C. Treat these as guides, not predictions.

Limits

A single reading has no time dimension. The logged analysis adds one, but it is still a summary. IPI’s Days to Mould and the VTT mould index follow wetness history more closely than the run analysis does, so check any decision against the raw series. Mould also needs spores and something to grow on, and these curves describe humidity only. Exports carry a note naming the model forms used.

Equations and sources
  • Dew point and RH: Magnus form, γ = 17.62·x / (243.12 + x) with x in °C. Dew point cannot exceed air temperature, so RH is capped at 100%.
  • PI: IPI’s form of the Arrhenius rate fitted to cellulose triacetate (Reilly 1995, as set out by Padfield 2004): k = RH × 5.9×10¹² × exp(−90300 / (8.314·TK)), PI = 1/k years. At 20 °C and 50% RH it gives about 42 years where IPI’s own tool showed 44, so treat it as the same model family, not a replica. IPI’s rate rises in direct proportion to RH. Where one lifetime form is needed, this tool uses CCI’s default instead (Michalski’s RH power of 1.3).
  • Lifetime multipliers: equations from CCI’s explanation of its lifetime calculator, each set to 1 at 20 °C and 50% RH: Sebera 1994 (lifetime in direct proportion to RH, at 125 kJ/mol; his activation energies are above currently accepted values, so his charts overstate the benefit of cold storage), Reilly et al. 1995 (acetate film), Michalski 2000 (RH power of 1.3, adjustable activation energy, used for the headline figure), Strlič et al. 2015 (paper, 119 kJ/mol) and Tétreault et al. 2023 (modern printing paper, adjustable activation energy). Checked against CCI’s published table of RH effects at 20 °C.
  • Moisture content: Hailwood–Horrobin sorption model with Simpson (1998, USDA Forest Products Laboratory) coefficients for wood. Paper and textile differ in absolute value, so read it as an index of strain, not a measurement. The bands are IPI’s.
  • Sedlbauer mould limit (LIM 0): RH = 100 × (0.02633·cosh(0.10083·(T − 30)) + 0.7153). Reference only.
